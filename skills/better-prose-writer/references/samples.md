# Samples: what the moves look like

Ten paragraphs by different writers, collected in October 2026 as short
excerpts for study. They do not share a voice. Read them for the moves named in
SKILL.md: the first sentence is already the point, the claim is flat, every
paragraph has something you could check, sources are named, sentence length
swings, hedges are specific, and the asides are ones a person would risk.

---

Typically, threats to American chestnut groves include pests, climate change,
and the fungus that destroyed their ancestors. Today, however, a new threat has
entered the picture: data centers. Near the bank of the Ohio River, a historic
American chestnut grove in Mason County, West Virginia has become the epicenter
of a fight against a planned 2,250 acre data center campus.

*Moves: a list of known things, then the new one. County, state, river and
acreage in the third sentence.*

---

Forecasting by the indispensable researchers at Africapolis suggests that by
2050 Africa will be home to by far the largest urban population in the world.
Even if these projections are exaggerated, it will be very surprising if
Africa's urban population does not exceed that of China and India by hundreds
of millions of people by the mid-century. This is a truly spectacular
transformation in world society as we have hitherto known it. The story of
human life in towns and cities that began with the neolithic will enter a new
chapter in which Africa is the center of the largest and most extended urban
settlements and civilizations. Given expected population trends beyond 2050,
Africa will likely retain that status into the 22nd century.

*Moves: a named source in the first sentence. A specific hedge ("even if these
projections are exaggerated") that then raises the stakes instead of lowering
them. Long sentences that earn their length. "Indispensable" is an opinion the
writer owns.*

---

In this trade war, remember that the sharpest weapon aimed at us isn't a
tariff. It's disinformation. Case in point: Trump keeps insisting America
doesn't need us. But Canada is the number one customer for more than two dozen
US states and supplies almost all of the natural gas the US imports. Turn that
tap off and see how "we don't need Canada" ages.

*Moves: the claim in two sentences, the second one three words long. "Case in
point:" as the connective. Two checkable numbers. A closing line the writer
would say out loud.*

---

Not every genre gets this kind of coordinated effort. Poets are probably
laughing. Nonfiction also seems curiously detached from these trappings. One
might reasonably assume the phenomenon would transfer across prose forms, but
little parallel infrastructure exists. There isn't, for example, the welcome
wagon of a "Memoir: Debut" label on Publishers Marketplace. Nor are there
awards for being young.

*Moves: a four-word aside in the second sentence. A particular ("Memoir:
Debut" on Publishers Marketplace) that proves the general claim. The last
sentence is six words and dry.*

---

"Are we falling out of love with nonfiction?" asked a much-circulated Guardian
piece published late last year. Data from a NielsenIQ report found the UK's
nonfiction print sales had dipped by 6 percent. "Speaking to publishing
insiders and readers," wrote Emma Loffhagen, "one word that cropped up
repeatedly was escapism." Two weeks later, the New York Times confirmed that
"Dragons, Sex, and the Bible" were the top categories that drove US book sales
in 2025. The world is on fire; why not sit back and watch some mythical
creatures, and also, I guess, cousins, get it on?

*Moves: four named sources in five sentences, two of them quoted. A number
with its source. One joke, at the end, that only this writer would make.*

---

Billionaires should not be making decisions critical to national security.
That's straightforward. And the case of Elon Musk and his attempt to undermine
Ukraine makes that clearer than ever. Recent events, that is in the past 24
hours, really don't change that point. If anything, they reinforce it.

*Moves: the claim is the first sentence. "That's straightforward" is the
writer refusing to hedge. "If anything" as the connective. The writer
corrects their own vagueness mid-sentence ("that is in the past 24 hours").*

---

Data centers cannot just use any kind of water either; it has to be clean
enough to ensure the equipment and pipes are not damaged. Tapping into public
drinking water is both easier and cheaper than paying the cost of treatment
themselves. That means data centers end up competing with communities over
their potable water supply.

*Moves: a mechanism explained one step per sentence, each step a fact the
reader could check (the water must be clean, treatment costs money, public
water is cheaper). "That means" carries the reader to the conclusion. No
adjective does the work; the chain does.*

---

If there's a theme that runs through my work and writing on this site, it's
the interplay between the shift towards agile thinking and the technical
patterns and practices that make agile software development practical. While
specifics of technology change rapidly in our profession, fundamental
practices and patterns are more stable. So writing about these allows me to
have articles on this site that are several years old but still as relevant
as when they were written.

*Moves: a writer naming the pattern in their own work, in first person,
because it is their work. A claim (fundamentals outlast specifics) followed
by the consequence the writer can vouch for (articles years old and still
relevant). "So" as the connective.*

---

Parkinson's changed my cognitive processing. Not all at once. A word that
used to surface instantly now takes a beat longer. A thread of argument that
once held itself in working memory needs an external anchor. Sixty years of
consequence-bearing experience didn't disappear. The pattern recognition I
built across careers in software development, management consulting, and
organizational leadership stayed. What changed was the biological system I'd
always run it through.

*Moves: a four-word first sentence and a three-word second one. The symptoms
are particular (a word that takes a beat longer, a thread that needs an
anchor), not a label. "Sixty years" and three named careers. The last
sentence makes the distinction the whole paragraph was built for.*

---

Practitioner authority rests on consequence-bearing experience, not
credential. Academic authority comes from method and institutional
affiliation. Thought leadership authority comes from platform size and
reputation management. Practitioner authority comes from having been
responsible for an outcome, not studying it, not advising on it from a safe
distance, but owning what happened next. That weight shows in every sentence
or it doesn't show at all. You can't install it and you can't fake it. This
holds whether you write about surgery, software, organizational change, or
bridge design. The field is incidental. The relationship to consequence is
not.

*Moves: a taxonomy stated flat, three kinds of authority in three sentences,
so the reader can disagree with the sorting. Four named fields instead of
"any field". The last two sentences are five words and six words, and they
are the point.*

## Before and after

A model's opening, and the author's own version of the same opening.

Before:

> In today's fast-paced world, maintaining focus in a home office environment
> can be a multifaceted challenge. It's important to note that leveraging the
> right systems and tools can revolutionize your productivity and unlock your
> true potential as a work-from-home entrepreneur.

After:

> The biggest focus killer in a home office has more to do with a lack of
> systems and less to do with a cluttered desk. Once I built a morning block
> routine and stopped checking email before noon, my personal output doubled.

The first version announces, uses nouns that fit any article (environment,
systems, tools, potential), and never commits to a position a reader could
disagree with. The second opens on a claim with a shape, names two habits, and
ends on a number. All three came from the author.
