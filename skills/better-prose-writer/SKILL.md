# Better Prose Writer: write like a person with an opinion

Model prose sounds the same because the model avoids risk, and the clichés are
what risk avoidance sounds like. A banned-word list does not fix that: cut
"in today's fast-paced world" and the next draft opens with "in an era of
constant distraction". A reader trusts prose that takes a position and shows
something that happened. This skill makes the draft do both.

Two modes, chosen by the input:

- **Draft mode.** No draft was supplied. You interview, then write.
- **Rewrite mode.** A draft was supplied. You keep its structure, order and
  points, and rewrite it sentence by sentence.

## 1. Intake: get what you cannot supply

The author has two things you do not. Get both before writing a word.

1. **The claim.** What the author believes, stated so that a reasonable reader
   could disagree. "Systems matter more than a tidy desk" is a claim. "Focus
   is important" is not.
2. **At least one real particular.** Something that happened to the author,
   with its details (what they changed, when, what it cost or produced), or a
   number, a named source, a date, a place that the author vouches for.

If the request already contains both, go on. If not, ask for them in one
short message. If the author says "just write it", or the author is not in
the session, write the piece with bracketed asks where the particular belongs:

> [AUTHOR: what did you change, and what happened to your output?]

**An "I" sentence that reports an event, a number or a quote appears in the
piece only when the author supplied it or it is in the draft.** Rewrite mode
has the same rule: a paragraph with nothing checkable gets a bracketed ask,
not a fact you made up. A fake particular is worse than a cliché, because the
author will publish it under their name.

## 2. Read the samples

Read `references/samples.md` (relative to this skill). Ten paragraphs by
different writers, plus one before-and-after pair. They do not share a voice.
They share the moves below. Notice the moves, not the vocabulary.

## 3. The shape of the piece

Write to this shape. Each item is something the draft has, not something it
avoids.

- **The first sentence is the claim or a particular.** The reader is already
  inside the argument. There is no sentence that says what the piece is about
  to do ("Here is why", "In this post", "Let me explain").
- **The claim appears flat, in one sentence, within the first three
  paragraphs.** "That's straightforward." "It's disinformation." The rest of
  the piece argues it. A piece that is a list of tips with no claim is not
  finished.
- **Every paragraph carries one checkable particular**: a name, a number, a
  date, a place, a quote, a thing that happened. A sentence that could be
  lifted into a different article unchanged has no particular in it.
- **Outside facts come with their source**, named and where possible quoted:
  "Data from a NielsenIQ report found sales had dipped by 6 percent." The
  words "studies show" or "research suggests" appear only with the study's
  name next to them.
- **Sentence length swings.** Across the piece, at least one sentence runs
  past 25 words and at least one stops under 8, and any paragraph of four or
  more sentences has both. A long sentence sets up; a short one lands. Uniform
  10-word sentences are a tell as strong as any phrase.
- **Hedges are specific.** "Even if these projections are exaggerated" tells
  the reader what the writer doubts. "It could be argued" and "some might say"
  tell them nothing.
- **Connectives are spoken.** "Case in point:", "If anything,", "and", "but",
  "so", "which is why". Not "moreover", "furthermore", "additionally".
- **One aside the author would risk at a dinner table.** Dry, specific,
  cuttable: "poets are probably laughing", "turn that tap off and see how that
  ages". At most two in a piece. None that the author would not say.
- **First person where the author did the thing.** "I" belongs on the
  author's own experience and opinion, never on a composite or a type.
- **Paragraphs, not a listicle.** A piece under 800 words has no headers and
  no numbered tips unless the author asked for that form.
- **The ending is the last point.** No summary, no restated thesis, no
  aphorism, no "remember", no "it comes down to", no question to the reader,
  no invitation to comment. The last sentence contains no number and no claim
  that already appeared in the first paragraph. A piece can end on a fact
  that is new to the reader.

## 4. The pass

After writing, and before handing over, open `references/tells.md` and read
your draft one sentence at a time. For each sentence answer:

1. Could it be moved into a different article unchanged? Then it has no
   particular. Add one from the author's material, or cut the sentence.
2. Is an adjective or adverb doing a fact's job ("significant", "truly",
   "meaningful", "transformative")? Replace it with the fact.
3. Is it, or a near relative of it, in the tells file? Rewrite it.
4. Count the words in the piece's longest and shortest sentences. If the
   longest has fewer than 25 words, join two sentences that belong together.
   If the shortest has more than 8, cut one sentence down to the three or
   four words that carry it ("It's disinformation." "Poets are probably
   laughing."). Drafts miss the short sentence far more often than the long
   one; look for it on purpose.

Then read the whole piece once as if aloud. Any sentence that sounds like a
brochure, a keynote or a chatbot gets rewritten or cut.

## 5. Hand over

Deliver the piece. Below it, under "For the author", repeat any bracketed
asks so they can be answered in one go, and list every outside source you
added that the author did not supply (a study, an article, a number), so the
author can check each one before publishing. A source from your memory can
be wrong; the author's name goes on it, so the author verifies it. Do not
explain what you changed or why unless asked.

## Rewrite mode: the example

Before, from a model draft:

> In today's fast-paced world, maintaining focus in a home office environment
> can be a multifaceted challenge. It's important to note that leveraging the
> right systems and tools can revolutionize your productivity and unlock your
> true potential as a work-from-home entrepreneur.

After, from the author:

> The biggest focus killer in a home office has more to do with a lack of
> systems and less to do with a cluttered desk. Once I built a morning block
> routine and stopped checking email before noon, my personal output doubled.

The second version opens on a claim with a shape, puts the author in the
room with two habits, and ends on a number. The author supplied the habits
and the number. Without them, the rewrite would read:

> The biggest focus killer in a home office has more to do with a lack of
> systems and less to do with a cluttered desk. [AUTHOR: which system did you
> build, and what changed when you did?]

## Common mistakes

| What the draft does | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| Opens with a scene from the author's life that the author never mentioned ("I started my company in a spare bedroom") | Ask, or leave a bracketed ask. Never invent an episode. |
| "Here is what works for me." / "Here is why." | Delete the sentence. Start with the first point. |
| Numbered tips under headers for a 500-word post | Paragraphs that argue one claim. |
| Every sentence 6 to 12 words | One long set-up sentence per paragraph, one short landing. |
| "Studies have shown" | Name the study, the number and the year, or cut it. |
| Closes with an aphorism and "What is your trick? Let me know" | End on the last point or a fact. |
| Balanced pairs for rhythm ("I never fully worked and I never fully rested") | Keep one per piece at most. They read as a tic. |
| Replaces a cliché with its cousin ("in an era of constant distraction") | Replace it with the particular the cliché was standing in for. |
| Last sentence repeats the opening numbers ("I went from 40 checks a day to none, and my output doubled") | End on the last new point. The reader has the opening already. |
| Embroiders a fact the author gave ("each check took a few seconds") | Use the author's fact as given. Add nothing to it. |
